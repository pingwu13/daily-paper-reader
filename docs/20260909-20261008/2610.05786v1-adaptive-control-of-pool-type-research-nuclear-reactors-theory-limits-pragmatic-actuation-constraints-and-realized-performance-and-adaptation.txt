# Adaptive control of pool-type research nuclear reactors: theory limits, pragmatic actuation constraints, and realized performance and adaptation 

Dimitrios Pylorof Linyu Lin 

Abstract —Adaptive control is attractive for nuclear reactors expected to operate predictably with extensive automation or bounded autonomy under dynamic electric and nonelectric demands, even as reactor and actuator characteristics depart from their nominal values. Standard adaptive control results, however, assume fixed matched dynamics and an input applied as computed. In this work we evaluate a recent model reference adaptive control with linear quadratic regulation (MRAC–LQR) construction on the nonlinear point-kinetics model of Purdue University Reactor Number One (PUR-1), with a moving physical reference, nonlinear rod worth, rate and travel limits, quantization, deadband, and optional power-only state reconstruction. An exact sampled decomposition separates matched adaptation from observer, command processing, actuator realization, independent reactivity, and unmatched-dynamics residuals. Numerical results emphasize compatible reference trajectories, plant parameter variation, response to unexpected rod motion, and actuator duty. Across sampled plants, one unchanged nominal design yields closely aligned responses through adaptively calculated, plant-dependent actuation histories, illustrating both the premise and the theory limits of adaptation at a pool-reactor scale that can support follow-on experimental verification and progression toward higher-consequence nuclear applications. 

Index Terms —adaptive control, MRAC–LQR, nuclear reactor control, actuator constraints, point kinetics, load following 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Since the advent of nuclear power generation in 1951 in the desert of eastern Idaho, reactor feedback control has matured significantly, but proposed missions and requirements are chang-ing rapidly. New reactor concepts are being considered for re-mote sites, islanded grids, industrial process heat, non-baseload electricity generation, and applications that seek predictable operation with extensive automation or bounded autonomy and limited or no onsite staffing; naval and spacecraft propulsion represent notable frontiers. Their power demands may be unusually dynamic relative to the predominantly baseload operation that has characterized most nuclear deployments. In parallel, the reactor and actuators seen by the controller can change with operating conditions and service life through fuel burnup, poison inventory, temperature feedback, and rod worth [1]–[5]. While protection and safety systems remain independent of feedback control, a critical question remains: 

can the power control layer follow compatible trajectories,

reject disturbances, and remain predictable as both the mission and the true plant depart from the nominal design ?The premise of model reference adaptive control (MRAC) is familiar: select a stable reference behavior, measure how the actual system departs from it, and update controller parameters so that the plant behaves more like the reference. We use the recent Fisher–Annaswamy MRAC–LQR construction [6], which combines a stabilizing nominal linear quadratic regulator (LQR), a direct adaptive update, and an epoch-gated learn-and-redesign mechanism, while retaining the model-following premise of direct MRAC [7], [8]. A gap nevertheless emerges for any physical, hardware-actuated system: what happens when the reference moves, uncertainty is not fully matched, and the computed input must pass through a nontrivial actuator? We develop this work as both a complement to and a reality check on adaptive control as an implementable reactor control approach. Pool-type research reactors provide an intermediate platform for examining adaptive control in nuclear systems. They retain neutron kinetics, control rods, instrumentation, operating procedures, and independent protection without the scale and regulatory complexity of a commercial plant. PUR-1 also provides a digital control and cyber-physical setting intended for staged evaluation [9]. This role resembles NASA’s use of the AirSTAR subscale aircraft to confront adaptive flight control designs with sensing and actuation before higher-consequence deployment [10]. Adaptive control in nuclear systems has been studied through state and output feedback MRAC for reactor-power tracking under parametric uncertainty and external disturbances [11], [12], and through model-reference robust adaptive control of control-element drive mechanisms [13]. Related LQR-referenced adaptive control has also been applied to a glycol heat exchanger in an integrated energy system, representing balance-of-plant thermal equipment rather than reactor core dynamics [14]. The present work links these lines by evaluating core power adaptation through an explicit nonlinear, sampled, and constrained control rod interface. Our contributions are as follows: 1) We implement MRAC–LQR for PUR-1 load following and evaluate nonlinear and constrained actuation, quanti-zation, deadband, observer-based state reconstruction, and disturbances, including effects outside the assumptions of the underlying theory. arXiv:2610.05786v1 [eess.SY] 5 Oct 2026 2) We formulate an exact sampled decomposition that separates matched adaptation from moving-reference, observer, actuator, independent reactivity, and unmatched dynamics residuals. 3) Numerical evaluations cover direct and shaped maneu-vers, 30 plant cases with paired adaptive/fixed-nominal runs, unexpected motion of a second rod, power-only reconstruction, and the deadband trade between power precision and actuator duty. Together, the formulation and 30-case comparison illustrate a practical meaning of robustness for adaptive control. The nominal physical reference, target dynamics, initial LQR gain, adaptive law structure, actuator calibration, and related logic are fixed once. Each plant is then simulated independently from the same controller initialization, with online adaptation driven only by that plant’s observed transitions and delivered input. Despite different plant dynamics, the power trajectories remain closely aligned while the rod histories differ. Unlike a conventional plant family study with a fixed controller, the coefficients of each deployed plant are not used for plant-specific synthesis; the common adaptive architecture responds online to the plant encountered. This distinction is relevant as reactor parameters evolve over the life cycle. II. R EACTOR , R EFERENCE , AND CONTROLLER

C. Deadband, precision, and actuator duty 

Figure 3 first shows the complete 40% → 48% → 36% →

45% maneuver, which exercises both regulation directions over a wider operating range. The selected and no-deadband power responses are nearly identical and neither reaches a rate or travel limit. In the final 300 s, however, removing the deadband raises mileage from 0.4 to 22.2 cm. Over the full maneuver, mileage rises from 87.1 to 115.0 cm and reversals from 4 to 287, while RMSE falls from 9.62 × 10 −5 to 2.26 × 10 −5. After the persistent +2 cm RR displacement, both settings recover in 5 s, but mileage rises from 16.8 to 31.1 cm and reversals from 10 to 160 without the deadband. In (26), deadband intentionally holds vak fixed for subthreshold corrections, replacing exact small-signal matching by a neighborhood set by deadband and quantization. Delivered input regression avoids treating an undelivered request as plant input, and parameter projection keeps the adaptive coefficients bounded. The multilevel test then supplies the empirical check: no accumulating error, delayed large correction, or escalating rod excursion appears, while quantization-driven switching is strongly suppressed. The mileage and reversal reductions matter because otherwise negligible corrections add motor and drivetrain duty, maintenance exposure, and sensitivity to backlash, stiction, wear, and failure.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we showed how MRAC–LQR can operate with nonlinear reactor dynamics and constrained actuation, including saturation, speed limits, and quantization. The plant family results demonstrate direct adaptation within prescribed bounds while retaining the nominal LQR design, reference construction, and actuator logic. This separation makes the scope of online change explicit for subsequent verification and regulatory review, although bounded coefficients alone do not establish stability of the complete constrained loop. The pool research reactor setting provides a path from simulation to experimental demonstrations and broader consideration in advanced reactor applications.
